# The Station

**Setting proposal · For review**

On the afternoon train, a parcel arrives addressed to a station that no longer appears on the map. A brother and sister recognize the name from an old sign. Then they find a photograph of that very sign above their own station entrance, taken this week.

The siblings grew up waiting with Pitsi, their family dog, for their father to finish work at this rural station. They know who sits by the café window, which bell belongs to the ticket counter, and how the village square sounds just after a train comes in. The player gets to use that familiarity to spot changes, follow leads and figure out what happened. The station is somewhere the siblings belong, and each arrival brings somebody or something they have yet to understand.

## Direction and decisions

The requested change is a game centered on a railway station in a rural village, with the setting reviewed before the game is rebuilt. The attached *Station-Setting-Concept.pdf* records the brother and sister, their father working at the station, the ages 9–14 audience, humor, educational mini-games and a longer detective story as the working direction. Its suggested map, roles, mechanics and opening case are creative proposals. The specifics below are proposed for this review. The existing playable prototype provides a baseline for Hebrew learning and interaction.

| Decision | Proposed for the first story |
| --- | --- |
| Where and when | A fictional Hebrew-speaking village among orchards and low hills. Contemporary everyday life, with old station furniture and records still in use where they serve a purpose. The country and exact year can wait. |
| What feels strange | Coincidences and apparently impossible details with grounded explanations. The first case can settle this tone before any decision about genuine fantasy. |
| Who the player is | One player directs the siblings as a pair. Both offer ideas, spot evidence and get things wrong. The opening can show their differing approaches through dialogue and the leads they suggest. |
| Why they are there | Dad works the public counter and handles parcel handovers on his shifts. He can confirm a record or help the kids reach a witness. The siblings make the connection between things Dad has had no reason to compare. |
| What matters | A village event, somebody's reputation, or a promise made years ago can carry a case. The first story asks whose work gets remembered at the station anniversary. |

## A place to come back to

| Place | What the siblings know | What the player can do there |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Station hall and public platform | The board changes before the afternoon arrival. Dad leaves a space on the windowsill for their bags. | Compare notices and public timetables, ask Dad about a parcel entry, or watch the arrival from the platform. |
| Station café | The owner can recall who ordered toast from where the plates were left. The station clock is visible from her usual spot. | Compare a witness's words with a photograph and ask where someone was standing. |
| Village square | The short walk from the station climbs past orchard walls into a square shared by shops and community events. Anniversary preparations change what is on the noticeboard. | Meet the organizer, check a public event notice and see the result of a case reflected in the village. |
| Workshop and former station building | The workshop fixes objects from around the village. A public footpath leads to the former building, which holds records and is separated from the active railway. | In later chapters, inspect a repaired object at the bench or look through material an adult has made available. |

The opening uses three stops: the station hall, the café and the square. The station clock and arrivals mark story beats that advance when the player chooses to move on. A returning player should be able to tell what changed from a few readable details, such as a revised sign or a familiar person at a different table. Children investigate in public spaces and ask for help with staff records. Railway operations belong to staff.

## The people

- **The siblings.** One loves a theory that can be tested immediately. The other remembers the exact words someone used and catches exceptions, sometimes after everyone is ready to move on. Either can have the decisive idea. In the opening, one spots that the sign and parcel share a name. The other proposes comparing the entrance with the photo, then gets carried away by a theory about a second station. Names, ages and which sibling tends toward which approach remain open.
- **Dad.** He recognizes the retired name and assumes an old address was copied. When the children bring him the photograph, he takes the question seriously and shows them the parcel's arrival entry. His shift keeps him at the counter while they follow the lead across the square.
- **Pitsi.** The siblings' charcoal-and-tan dog knows the walk between the station and the square and thinks the café bench is reserved for him. He helps the siblings notice details on public paths. They decide which observations belong in the notebook.
- **The postman.** The existing postman has a regular stop at the station hall. He collects village mail and helps assemble the anniversary postcard rack in his mini-game.
- **The café owner.** She photographed the sign trial from her café doorway. Her account of seeing someone carry a bundle is exact about the bundle and vague about the person's face. She still proudly displays a certificate for **הכריך שהשתפר הכי הרבה**.
- **The anniversary organizer.** She helps hang the old sign for a photo, arranges the display in the square and needs a real answer before printing the exhibit caption. She knows who contributed to the display and can show the kids the preview schedule.

Jokes should sound like people who know each other. A sibling can earn a laugh by presenting a grand theory about a second station and then holding the café photo against the crooked tile they both trip over. The sandwich certificate is there for anyone who wants to ask about it. Feedback on a mistaken deduction points to something the player can check.

## What the player does

Notice a discrepancy, choose a question, follow a lead and use what you find to test an explanation. The shared notebook keeps the photograph, label and other observations close to witness statements. Each deduction asks the player to connect evidence to a claim. An incomplete explanation stays editable and free help can point to an overlooked source, compare two details side by side or demonstrate the connection.

**Why might this be fun?** Early evidence should make the player want to know more. A good deduction changes what they think they saw and prompts an immediate response from someone they know. The station can also reward curiosity with optional interactions, such as reading an old notice or asking what happened to the original sandwich.

Hebrew, RTL, a desktop browser and a physical Hebrew keyboard are the existing baseline. The opening proposes one meaningful Hebrew word task tied to a parcel record. Copying with support is typing practice. Independent spelling needs a separate prompt with the answer out of view and separate reporting. The evidence and story stay available through free assistance. The [opening case](rural-station-opening-case.md) makes that contract concrete.

Two references inform the proposal:

- *Duck Detective: The Secret Salami* connects evidence inspection to deductions in a comic mystery. The station version uses short explanations supported by visible evidence so the child can point to what changed their mind. [Happy Broccoli Games](https://www.happybroccoligames.com/)
- *Railbound* pairs a relaxed main path with harder optional puzzles. The station version would offer a readable main trail and optional competing explanations for players who want to pursue them. [Afterburn's Railbound page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1967510/Railbound/)

## How the longer story could grow

The opening ends with the correct object delivered and a display caption the village can stand behind. Its map gives the siblings the next question. A public path appears in two drawings with different names, and people in the village remember who drew it differently. A second chapter could settle where the path went, and a later chapter could establish whose work was left out of the public record. The connected mystery is how the village came to tell its own story. New arrivals bring new evidence to the same familiar hub. This is a proposed direction for the larger arc.

## For review

The decisions that change the feel of the game most are the contemporary village, the siblings' partnership as the player's point of view, and a grounded mystery about the village's own history. The written opening is a way to judge whether those choices create curiosity. The final title, character names and ages, wider family, geography and full chapter plan remain open. The [agreed art direction](rural-station-art-direction.md) describes the visual style and humor. Setting approval comes before rebuilding the game.
